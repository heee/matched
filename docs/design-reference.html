1Matched — first pass
What I assumed, and why
Carried over from Across
Invite-by-link rooms, live sync, contribution-style rankings, daily puzzle, streaks. Same 5-tab bottom nav shape: Home · Play · + · Ranking · You. Sentence case throughout.
New visual identity
Tile-first: felt table, warm wood rail, real bone tiles with a physical edge. Instrument Serif for the wordmark, Figtree for UI. Two board colors max (felt green, night slate for low light).
Board math
Whole board always fits, never pans. That caps a phone layout at roughly 8 columns × 5 rows per layer — so the classic 144-tile turtle is a tablet layout. On iPhone the catalog tops out near 72 tiles, which also serves easy mode.
Tile art
Faces here are placeholders built from type and geometry. Every face is a slot sized 40×52 at 1× — drop real art in and nothing else moves. Flowers and seasons are marked as empty slots.
Board 1f is live — tap two matching free tiles. The other players clear tiles on their own while you watch.

Board — shared, free-for-all
  • 52 tiles across 2 layers. The whole board always fits; it never pans or zooms.
  • Tap a free tile to select, tap its match to clear. Blocked tiles are dimmed, desaturated, and reject the tap.
  • Four score cards on top show pairs taken. At two or more in a row the player’s name is replaced by their streak.
  • Cleared pairs fly to the tray and stay tinted with the taker’s color — permanent attribution, the mahjong equivalent of Across’s per-letter coloring.
  • The multi-spark stroke icon gives a hint; adjacent controls shuffle, undo, and toggle a live count of currently playable free matching pairs. Assist usage counts against leaderboard credit.
  • Reactions fire from the bottom right and float over the felt; a toast announces another player’s take, outlined in their color.
  • Simulated players stay idle until your first match so the board is intact when you arrive.
  • Table backgrounds under consideration: felt green (shown) as the default; night slate, used here for race mode, as a low-light option; and tier-unlocked felts — deep felt at Jade, a wood-rail table at Rosewood. Cap is two colors in play at once so tiles stay the only source of color.
Score cards along the top, tray along the bottom. Free tiles carry a faint gold edge; blocked tiles sit dimmer and refuse the tap.

Board — race mode
  • Same layout, separate boards. Nobody can take a tile out from under you.
  • Night-slate felt instead of green so the mode is obvious in a screenshot or a glance.
  • Live progress bars, one per player, ordered by position rather than by seat.

  • No tray: your cleared pairs are implied by your own bar.

Room setup
  • One scrollable screen, never a wizard — the target is under a minute from tap to invite.
  • Mode is the first question because it changes the board, the scoring, and the presence model. Three options: shared, race, solo — solo still reports into Ranking and the daily streak.
  • Layout opens the catalog; difficulty presets set tile count (36 / 52 / 72) and double as easy mode.
  • Toggles cover the easy-mode affordances (free-tile glow, hints) and link visibility. Solo hides Players and link visibility; disabling hints also removes Hint/open-pair controls and idle clues.
  • Shared and Race use Create & invite and open the invite sheet. Solo uses Create and play and enters the board directly.

Daily puzzle
  • One board for everyone, one attempt, resets on a 24h clock.
  • The streak lives here rather than on Home, so it is tied to the habit that earns it.
  • Results contain only registered humans who completed today's board; there are no simulated bot finishes.
  • After the active player finishes, Home shows only View results and the Daily screen removes its disabled play button.
  • The active player's result is written locally before network sync, so it appears immediately even while offline.
  • Times stay hidden until you finish — the one place I argued against pure competition.
  • Finishing also feeds the same metrics Ranking sorts by.
The streak lives here, not on Home. Group times are blurred until you play — the one place I'd argue against pure competition.
